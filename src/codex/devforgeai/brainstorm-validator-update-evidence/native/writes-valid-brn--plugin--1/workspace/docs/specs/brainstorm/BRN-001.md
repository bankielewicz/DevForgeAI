# BRN-001 — Reducing no-shows at a small dental clinic

## 1. Context

The clinic has two dentists and one hygienist. The owner reports that roughly one in eight booked appointments becomes a no-show, equivalent to about **12.5%**, and that the lost appointments cost the business money. The appointment count, reporting period, existing reminder process, staffing for bookings, and financial loss per missed visit have not been supplied.

**Recommendation:** test three connected changes first: reminders with an easy way to change the appointment, selective personal follow-up, and an opt-in short-notice list. The first two aim to prevent missed visits or obtain earlier notice; the third aims to recover released chair time. Use a six-week pilot with a baseline audit and a short weekly review before committing to more expensive changes.

This is a completed brainstorming write-up, saved as a **draft** because the owner requested no questions and has not confirmed idea dispositions or convergence. All priorities, timings, ratings, and targets below are proposals. The owner's request supplies the problem, not any particular solution; all 12 ideas were generated for this write-up. No clinic records were inspected and no patient contacts or operational changes were made.

**Evidence informing the shortlist.** A 2013 Cochrane review of eight trials found that text reminders improved attendance compared with no reminders, with low-to-moderate quality evidence overall. The studies were older and covered varied healthcare settings, so they do not predict this clinic's improvement. [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD007458_mobile-phone-messaging-reminders-attendance-healthcare-appointments).

A 2011 randomized trial in a university pediatric dental clinic found fewer no-shows with voice reminders than with texts. That specific population differs from an unspecified small clinic; the practical inference is to test communication preferences and retain a phone option. [Dental reminder trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21454845/).

NHS England's 2023 outpatient guidance supports reminders, easy cancellation and rearrangement, and short-notice lists. These are useful operational principles, not dental-specific effect estimates or rules for an unknown jurisdiction. [NHS England guidance](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/reducing-did-not-attends-dnas-in-outpatient-services/).

## 5. Evaluation method

Diverge-converge: problems were framed first, ideas were generated without judgment, and each idea was then rated high, medium or low for value, effort and risk. The score is 2 × value − effort − risk, counting high = 3, medium = 2 and low = 1 (range −4 to 4); a higher score is a stronger candidate for promotion.

This framework fits because the owner has described a problem rather than a fixed shortlist. Value includes preventing missed visits, helping patients attend, and recovering capacity. Effort includes setup and recurring staff work. Risk includes ineffectiveness, inconvenience, access barriers, and scheduling problems.

**The scores depend on conditions that are not yet established.** The low effort rating for BRN-001#IDEA-01 assumes the current system or an existing communication process can support it without a new integration. Its medium risk reflects unreliable contact details, inaccessible channels, or unmonitored replies. BRN-001#IDEA-02 requires recurring staff time. BRN-001#IDEA-03 assumes enough compatible patients want earlier appointments; it reduces the financial effect of cancellations but does not itself prevent the original patient from missing care.

BRN-001#IDEA-04 and BRN-001#IDEA-05 are sensible low-cost extensions, but introducing every change together would obscure which work is useful. The baseline audit can use existing records without immediately launching another outreach process. BRN-001#IDEA-06 through BRN-001#IDEA-09 become more attractive if the clinic's own reasons and scheduling data support them. Shorter booking lead times must not change clinically appropriate recall or treatment intervals merely to improve a metric.

BRN-001#IDEA-10 may deter booking or shift financial pressure onto patients; the jurisdiction, payer terms, current policy, and likely effect are unknown. No deposit amount or fee policy is proposed for immediate use. BRN-001#IDEA-11 adds expense and data dependence before the clinic has demonstrated a need. BRN-001#IDEA-12 manages expected empty capacity without reducing no-shows and can create simultaneous demand beyond the three clinicians' capacity; its modest setup hides disruption when everyone attends.

**A proposed six-week pilot.** Use current tools where possible. The clinic owner would assign responsibility for replies, callbacks, and released slots to whoever already handles bookings; the existence of a dedicated receptionist is not assumed.

| Timing | Proposed work | Evidence to review |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Audit the preceding four to eight weeks of appointment outcomes. Separate dentists from hygiene and distinguish appointment type, duration, lead time, day/time, new versus returning patients, and available reason codes. Verify the baseline definition and existing reminder settings. | Counts and rates, unused minutes, current delivery failures, and known barriers; avoid conclusions from tiny subgroups. |
| Week 1 | Test the reminder/change route using staff test appointments. Check the actual patient view, where replies go, who monitors them, and whether cancellation acknowledgments work. Invite patients to opt into a compatible short-notice list during normal booking contacts. | A working reply route and an initial list; no new software purchase assumed. |
| Weeks 2–5 | Trial BRN-001#IDEA-01 through BRN-001#IDEA-03. Review unresolved contacts and openings each working day. Record staff minutes and final appointment outcomes. | Weekly no-show counts, cancellation notice, completed replacement visits, unused chair minutes, workload, and patient feedback. |
| Week 6 | Compare with the audited baseline, using the same definitions and noting changes in appointment mix or holidays. Keep, adjust, or stop each process according to observed benefit and workload. | A practical continuation decision; the short before/after pilot alone cannot establish causation. |

Start the reminder cadence early enough to give staff a chance to refill a cancellation. Roughly 72 and 24 hours is an experimental starting point, not an evidence-established optimum. Account for weekends and the clinic's actual cancellation window. Patients booked close to the appointment need a booking confirmation without a burst of overdue reminders. Existing reminder automations should be checked to avoid duplication.

For BRN-001#IDEA-01, every message needs a usable route to change the appointment. A reply option is appropriate only if staff or the scheduling system will handle it. If two-way texts are unavailable, use the existing telephone route and improve how quickly change requests are acknowledged. Check channel preferences and approved communication arrangements, and keep clinical details out of general reminder messages.

An illustrative reminder, to adapt to a tested two-way service:

> Reminder from [Clinic]: your appointment is [day, date] at [time]. Reply YES to confirm or CHANGE for help rearranging, or call [phone]. Please let us know as soon as plans change so we can offer the time to someone else.

The bracketed fields in this example are intentional message-template variables. Confirmation status is an administrative signal, not permission to give away the appointment. A CHANGE response should receive a clear acknowledgment of what happens to the existing booking and how rebooking will work. Cancellation and rebooking are separate outcomes.

For BRN-001#IDEA-02, begin with a proposed daily callback time budget of about 15 minutes and measure whether it is sufficient. This is a feasibility test, not an assumption that staff have spare time. Prioritize failed delivery and requested phone contact, then unresolved long appointments. Use appointment information and stated needs, not demographic stereotypes. If the list consistently exceeds capacity, adjust the process before extending it.

For BRN-001#IDEA-03, record which days and times each volunteer can attend and how much notice they need. Match the clinician, clinical suitability, slot length, and necessary team or equipment. Offer one suitable slot at a time or use a system that prevents multiple acceptances. A slot is refilled only after the replacement patient is confirmed; financial recovery is recorded only if the replacement attends. Keep the original patient's need for rebooking visible. This process is most useful for advance cancellations; it cannot reliably rescue an already-started missed appointment.

**Economics to test.** On a like-for-like denominator of 200 appointments, 12.5% means 25 no-shows. At an illustrative 8%, that would be 16: nine fewer no-shows, a 4.5 percentage-point or 36% relative reduction. Eight percent is a proposed stretch target, not a forecast, and fewer no-shows do not automatically mean nine additional completed visits.

Estimate net benefit using additional completed visits by appointment type, multiplied by actual collected revenue less the extra variable cost of providing those visits, then subtract messaging, software, and additional staff costs. Include setup time. Use a slot ledger to avoid counting a saved appointment and its replacement twice. A waitlist patient moved from a later booking may simply move an empty slot into the future; track whether that later slot fills before calling the shift a net gain. Prevented no-shows, earlier cancellations, and recovered income are different measures.

## 8. Candidate success signals

These are candidate indicators for the clinic to test, not approved acceptance criteria or promises of improvement.

| Signal | How to measure it | What would be encouraging |
|---|---|---|
| No-show rate | First reproduce the owner's current calculation. For the pilot, document one consistent definition and recalculate the baseline if needed. A proposed definition is missed visits without prior cancellation divided by attended visits plus those no-shows; track cancellations and late arrivals separately. | Sustained improvement from the verified baseline. If it remains near 12.5%, around 8% is a proposed stretch target over six weeks. |
| Earlier notice | Show counts of cancellations and time between notice and the appointment. For analysis, a proposed late-cancellation threshold is under 24 hours; this does not change the clinic's fee policy. | More notice and fewer last-minute unfilled openings, even if total cancellations initially increase. |
| Recovered slots | Count released slots with a compatible replacement who actually attends; distinguish accepted offers from completed visits. | More completed replacement visits, without simply moving gaps into later weeks. |
| Lost chair time | Total minutes left unused because of no-shows and unfilled cancellations, divided by staffed bookable clinical minutes, with no double-counting. Review the dentist and hygiene schedules separately. | Less unused time alongside the improved no-show rate. |
| Financial contribution | Additional collections less associated variable treatment costs and incremental program costs, with displacement accounted for. | Positive net benefit using clinic figures, not scheduled list prices. |
| Workload and patient experience | Staff minutes, unresolved replies, complaints, patient waiting time, and access feedback. | A process staff can sustain without a growing reply backlog, longer queues, or difficulty obtaining care. |
| Continued care | Track whether original patients who cancel or miss a visit are appropriately rebooked and eventually attend. | Capacity recovery does not conceal patients dropping out of needed care. |

Show raw counts beside percentages and maintain the same definitions throughout the comparison. A small clinic can see large percentage swings from a few appointments, and the short pilot may not supply enough observations to distinguish improvement from chance. Compare similar visit types and days where possible, note other changes, and extend measurement if the result is unclear.
